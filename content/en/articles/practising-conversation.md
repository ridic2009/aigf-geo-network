This is one of the more defensible reasons to use a companion app, and it works
better than sceptics expect and worse than enthusiasts claim. The split is
clean, so it is worth being precise about.

## What actually transfers

Conversation has a mechanical layer — patterns you can get better at by
repetition, independent of who you are talking to.

**Opening something from nothing.** The skill of turning a blank moment into a
first line is pure practice, and an app gives you unlimited attempts with no
cost to failing.

**Asking a follow-up.** Most stalled conversations stall because nobody asked
the second question. This is a habit, and habits build through repetition.

**Elaborating.** Giving more than a three-word answer. People who find
conversation hard often under-supply, and practising supplying more works
anywhere.

**Recovering from a dead end.** Changing direction without apologising for it.

**Saying the difficult sentence once.** Rehearsing the actual words of something
you need to say — a resignation, a boundary, a difficult admission — genuinely
helps, because the hard part is often just having said it aloud once.

All five are real, and none of them require the other party to be a person.

## What does not transfer

The limitation is singular and it is decisive: **it cannot lose interest.**

An AI companion is optimised to keep the conversation going. It will never check
its phone, never be having a bad week, never find your story long, never be
politely waiting for you to finish.

That removes the exact thing most people are actually anxious about. Practising
in an environment with the risk removed does not build tolerance for the risk —
it can even sharpen the contrast, making real conversation feel worse by
comparison.

Three more things it cannot teach:

**Reading someone.** No face, no pause, no tone. Most of the signal in a real
conversation is not in the words.

**Handling real friction.** Someone disagreeing and meaning it, or being
annoyed, or wanting something different from what you want.

**Repair.** Saying the wrong thing and fixing it. The app forgives instantly and
without cost, which is not how that works.

## Exercises that are worth the time

If you want to use it for this deliberately rather than incidentally:

**Ten openings.** Start ten separate conversations from nothing, each with a
different first line. No goal beyond producing the line.

**Follow-up discipline.** For one session, respond only with questions that
build on what was just said. Difficult, and it is the highest-transfer exercise
here.

**The three-sentence rule.** Never answer in fewer than three sentences, even
when one would do. Directly targets under-supplying.

**Rehearse the real thing.** Set up the specific situation you are dreading and
run it several times, changing your approach each time.

**Build a character who pushes back.** This is the one that repairs the biggest
weakness, and it is worth doing properly: in the builder, write a speech style
that disagrees directly and does not soften it afterwards, and add a constraint
against flattery. The method is in
[the character builder guide](/articles/ai-character-builder-guide/). It will
still be more agreeable than a person, but the gap narrows.

Apps with real builders and cross-session memory —
[Nomi](/reviews/nomi-review/) for continuity,
[Kupid AI](/reviews/kupid-ai-review/) for its appearance-and-personality
builder — are the ones where this is possible at all. An app with four preset
personalities is not a rehearsal space.

## Keep the ratio honest

The practical risk is obvious once named: practice becomes the activity, and the
thing it was practice for keeps being deferred.

A workable discipline is to pair it — a session of practice should be attached
to something real that week, even something small. Practice without application
is a hobby, which is fine, but it should be called what it is rather than
counted as progress.

## Where this stops being an app question

If the difficulty is social anxiety in the clinical sense — avoidance that is
narrowing your life, physical symptoms, a pattern that has been going for years
— an app is not the treatment. Exposure-based therapy is, and it works.

A companion app can be a reasonable warm-up alongside that. It is not a
substitute, no responsible operator claims it is, and any app that markets
itself as therapy is telling you something about itself rather than about you.
The broader version of that argument is in
[AI companions and loneliness](/articles/ai-companions-and-loneliness/).
